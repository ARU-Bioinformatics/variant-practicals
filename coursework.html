Coursework: the exome of a child with “Rienhoff syndrome”

Bea Rienhoff was born with features that overlap Marfan and Loeys–Dietz syndromes but fitted neither. Her father, a physician and biologist, spent years searching for the cause, and in 2013 her exome sequence identified it. You will repeat the analysis: call variants in her exome yourself, filter and annotate them to find the likely cause, check the evidence in the reads, and show the affected position in the protein structure.

Laboratory report · 2,000 wordsRead the coursework brief on Canvas
Patient data – please read

The BAM file is a real patient's sequence. Do not share it outside your studies at ARU and do not upload it to public servers. This workbench reads the file on your own computer: samtools, bcftools and IGV all run inside this web page, and the file is never uploaded. Only two things leave your computer, and only when you ask: pressing Annotate with VEP sends the positions and alleles of your filtered variants (no reads, no names) to Ensembl – the same as pasting a VCF into the VEP web page – and the database links in a variant's details open those websites with that variant's position or identifier.

Before you start

  • Read the news article (ReinhoffSyndromeNewsArticle.pdf, Maher 2013 Nature 498:418–419) and the coursework brief.
  • Download the coursework data from Canvas and unzip it. You need daughters-reads.bam and daughters-reads.bai (keep them together). The reads cover chromosomes 14 and 18, aligned to hg19.
  • Work through Reads to variants and Variants to meaning first: this workbench uses the same commands and tools.

A · Load and check your data

Step A1 · Load the files
  1. In the Data tab (or here), choose daughters-reads.bam and daughters-reads.bai together.
  2. In the Terminal, check they are there: ls -lh data
Step A2 · What is in the file?
  1. Look at the header: samtools view -H data/daughters-reads.bam | head -n 30. Which reference genome was used? What else does the header tell you about how the file was made?
  2. Look at a few alignments, from the first megabase of chromosome 14 that has reads: samtools view data/daughters-reads.bam chr14:19000000-20000000 | head -n 5. Note the read length, the FLAG, MAPQ, the quality string (read Box 1 of the brief) and the tags at the end (X0 = number of equally good places the read fits).
  3. Count the reads per chromosome: samtools idxstats data/daughters-reads.bam
  4. Summarise the flags: samtools flagstat data/daughters-reads.bam
  5. Coverage of the two chromosomes: samtools coverage -r chr14 data/daughters-reads.bam and the same for chr18. Why is the mean depth over a whole chromosome low for an exome?
Notes for Methods: reference build, read length, single- or paired-end, number of reads mapped to chr14 and chr18 (and elsewhere), % mapped.
Notes: what do you notice about the base qualities (Box 1 of the brief)? What does that mean for the QUAL values a variant caller will report, and what other evidence can you use to judge a call?

B · Call variants

The reads were aligned for you (with Galaxy). The alignments are only half the job: now make the list of places where Bea differs from the reference, with the same tools as in the practical.

Step B1 · Call
  1. Call variants on chromosomes 14 and 18 (this reads about a million alignments on each chromosome: allow a few minutes – the timer at the top of the terminal shows it is working):
    bcftools mpileup -Ou -a AD,DP -f ref/hg19.fa -r chr14,chr18 data/daughters-reads.bam | bcftools call -mv -Ov -o results/calls.vcf
  2. Count the calls: grep -c -v '^#' results/calls.vcf
Why -r chr14,chr18?

About 2% of the reads are placed on other chromosomes (you saw them with idxstats), but the reference provided here contains only chromosomes 14 and 18. The region option also tells you something useful: with an index, bcftools can jump straight to the parts of the file you ask for.

Step B2 · Filter
  1. Keep the confident calls, for example: bcftools view -i 'QUAL>=30 && INFO/DP>=10' results/calls.vcf > results/calls.filtered.vcf. You may choose different thresholds – think about Box 1 – but you must justify them.
  2. Count what is left.
  3. Keep copies: download results/calls.vcf results/calls.filtered.vcf

Useful extras Extension

  • Genotype counts: bcftools view -H -g het results/calls.filtered.vcf | wc -l (heterozygous; -g hom for homozygous).
  • A table of the key fields: bcftools query -f '%CHROM\t%POS\t%REF\t%ALT\t%QUAL\t%DP\t[%GT\t%AD]\n' results/calls.filtered.vcf | head
  • A summary: bcftools stats results/calls.filtered.vcf | grep -A 8 '^# SN'
  • Stricter mapping quality (reads placed uniquely): add -q 20 to mpileup and compare the numbers.
Notes: the exact commands you ran, the number of calls before and after filtering, and why you chose your thresholds.

C · Annotate with VEP

Step C1 · Load and annotate your calls
  1. Load your filtered VCF into the Variants tab.
  2. Press Annotate with VEP (GRCh37). With a few hundred variants this takes about a minute. If Ensembl is busy, try again a little later – or download your VCF and use the VEP web page on grch37.ensembl.org.
  3. Download the annotated table (Table button) for your supplementary information.
Notes: how many of your variants are in genes? How many change the protein (HIGH/MODERATE impact)? Record the date and the VEP settings for Methods (Ensembl VEP REST service, GRCh37, Ensembl release – shown in the Variants tab, canonical transcripts, gnomAD v4.1 frequencies, SIFT, PolyPhen-2, CADD, SpliceAI).

D · Filter and prioritise

Bea's condition is rare and severe, and her parents are unaffected. Use the funnel above the table and the filters to go from hundreds of calls to a few candidates – and be ready to justify every step.

  • Consequence: which kinds of variant could plausibly cause a severe developmental disorder? What might a consequence filter miss (think about CYP2C19*2)?
  • Frequency: how common could a causal variant be in gnomAD, given how rare the condition is?
  • Inheritance: with unaffected parents, is a heterozygous or a homozygous variant more likely? (What would you need to be sure?)
  • Genes: which of the remaining genes are known to be involved in connective tissue, growth or TGF-β signalling? Search OMIM and UniProt for each.
Step D1 · Build your funnel
  1. Build your funnel with the filters in the Variants tab – Impact (with or without + SpliceAI ≥ 0.5), Population frequency and Genotype. Each filter you set adds a bar to the funnel, which shows how many variants are left after each step. Try different choices and see what each one removes.
  2. Click each remaining candidate to open its details: gene, consequence, frequency, predictions and database links.
Notes: a table of your filtering steps (step, reason, number of variants left) and your list of candidates with a sentence on each.

E · Inspect the candidates in IGV

Step E1 · Look at the reads
  1. Show your BAM file in IGV: (or use Load files from my computer in the IGV tab).
  2. Go to each candidate: type its position (e.g. chr14:12,345,678) in IGV's search box, or press Show the reads in IGV in the variant's details.
  3. For each: the depth; the number of reads for each allele (click the coverage bar); both strands?; mapping quality; anything suspicious nearby. Is the call convincing?
  4. Zoom in to read the gene, exon, strand, the codon and amino acid, and their number (hover over the amino acids). Remember: for a gene on the minus strand, read the codon on the other strand, right to left.
  5. Save a clear image of each variant for your figures (Save image), and write a full legend.
Notes: for each candidate – chromosome, position, reference and alternative base, reads for each (and %), zygosity, gene, exon, strand, codon change and amino-acid change.

F · Interpret the candidates

For each candidate, gather the evidence – the details card in the Variants tab links to most of these:

QuestionWhere to look
Is it known? Has it been reported in patients?dbSNP (rs number), ClinVar, the literature (PubMed)
How common is it in healthy people? How often does the gene tolerate damaging variants?gnomAD (the website's v2.1 dataset uses GRCh37 positions; v4 uses GRCh38): allele frequency; the gene page's constraint scores (e.g. pLI/LOEUF for loss-of-function, missense Z)
What does the gene do? Which disorders is it linked to, and how are they inherited?OMIM, UniProt (Function, Disease & Variants), GeneCards
What does the change do to the protein?VEP consequence and HGVS; SIFT, PolyPhen-2, CADD, SpliceAI; the protein's domains and structure
For a stop-gained variant: will the mRNA be destroyed (nonsense-mediated decay) or a short protein made? Are other stop-gained variants in this gene common in healthy people?Where the stop falls (which exon, and how far from the last exon); the loss-of-function variants listed for the gene in gnomAD, and how common they are
Notes: the evidence for and against each candidate, with database identifiers (rs numbers, OMIM numbers) for your references.

G · Show the variant in the protein structure

Step G1 · Find and load a structure
  1. Find experimental structures of your protein: its UniProt entry, section Structure (or the PDB). Choose one that contains the region with your variant. (The coursework data folder also contains one structure file – which protein is it?)
  2. Type the four-character PDB code into the PDB ID box of the 3D viewer and press Load (or use Open file for a .pdb file from your computer).
  3. Identify the chains: which chain is your protein? Is there more than one copy, or a partner protein? Hover over the structure and use the workspace list.
Numbering (Box 2 of the brief)

Positions from IGV and VEP count from the first amino acid of the precursor protein made from the gene. Many secreted proteins are cut after translation, and structures of the mature protein are often numbered from its own first residue. Check UniProt's PTM/Processing section: if the mature chain starts at residue n, a precursor position p becomes p − (n − 1) in the structure. Always check that the residue you highlight is the expected amino acid.

Step G2 · Make the figure
  1. Select your residue with the viewer's command line, e.g. select A:123, then show sticks sele, color orange sele, label sele and center sele (use your own chain and number).
  2. Show what it touches: select near sele 4.5 then show sticks sele. Which atoms are within bonding distance – hydrogen bonds, salt bridges, disulfide bonds, contacts with another chain or a ligand? Hover over atoms to identify them.
  3. Add a key and labels, then save a high-resolution image (Save image tab). Write a legend that gives the PDB code, the chains shown and the numbering you used.
Notes: PDB code and resolution; chain(s); the residue number in the gene and in the structure; where the residue is (surface or core, structured or flexible, what it contacts); how the new amino acid differs (size, charge, bonds it can or cannot make); what that may do to the protein.
